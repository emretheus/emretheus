<Section n="02" label="The intuition" title="Meaning has coordinates.">

Here is the problem, concretely. Five sentences. Search for “cat”.

<Figure caption="Same corpus, same query — two different definitions of “match”.">
  <LexicalSemantic />
</Figure>

Keyword search matches strings. Semantic search matches meaning. That difference is not a trick — it is geometry.

Every word, sentence or document can be placed at coordinates in a meaning-space. Similar meanings end up near each other; unrelated meanings drift apart.

| word   | x    | y    |
| ------ | ---- | ---- |
| cat    | 0.8  | 0.7  |
| kitten | 0.75 | 0.72 |
| car    | −0.4 | 0.1  |

`cat` and `kitten` are almost on top of each other. `car` points somewhere else entirely. The numbers are made up; the geometry is not.

<Term def="A learned list of numbers (coordinates) that represents the meaning of a word, sentence or document. Similar meanings get similar numbers.">Embeddings</Term> are how this gets computed. You never write the coordinates by hand.

<Deeper>

Why would coordinates emerge at all? Because of a boring observation about language: words that appear in similar contexts tend to mean similar things. A model trained to predict context — the word before, the word after — is forced to give “puppy” and “dog” similar numbers, because they show up in the same places. Nobody programs the similarity in. It falls out of the statistics.

Two dimensions are a drawing convenience. A production embedding has hundreds or thousands of them. The geometry still works; you just can’t sketch it.

</Deeper>

</Section>

<Section n="03" label="The math" title="Do these two arrows point the same way?">

Forget the equation for a second. Two vectors are two arrows from the same origin. The standard question is: what is the cosine of the angle between them?

- **1** — same direction: the same thing, said twice.
- **0** — perpendicular: unrelated.
- **−1** — opposite: an antonym, which is a strange kind of similarity.

<Figure caption="Drag the ember vector. The angle — and the ranking — update as you move.">
  <VectorSpace />
</Figure>

Move your query towards `cat`: the similarity climbs towards 1, and the angle shrinks. Swing it left towards `car`: the leading match changes. That is the whole mechanism — no round trips, no network, just directions.

<Deeper>

The dot product at the centre of the formula measures projection: how much of one arrow lies along the other. Divide by both lengths and the lengths stop mattering — leaving only direction.

That design choice matters in practice. A two-page document and a one-line sentence about the same thing should match, even though one vector is “bigger”. Cosine similarity says: I don’t care how long or loud you are, only which way you point.

</Deeper>

<MathOnly>

For vectors u and v:

`cos(u, v) = (u · v) / (|u| · |v|)`

- `u · v` — multiply component-wise and sum: `0.6·0.8 + 0.55·0.7 = 0.865`.
- `|u|`, `|v|` — Euclidean lengths: `√(x² + y²)`.
- In real systems both vectors are usually **normalised** first (length 1), so the denominator becomes 1 and similarity is just the dot product.

With 768 dimensions the arithmetic is identical; only your ability to picture it runs out.

</MathOnly>

</Section>

The pipeline is short. The work is in the parts that don’t show: how documents were split before embedding, which model was chosen, what k is, and whether anyone measured the result.

<Deeper>

**Chunking decides what can ever be found.** Documents are cut into pieces before embedding. Cut a table away from its caption and no retriever can reunite them. Chunk size is a trade: small chunks are precise but context-poor; large chunks carry context but blur meaning.

**Approximate indexes are approximate.** HNSW-style graphs find almost all of the true nearest neighbours almost all of the time. For search that is an excellent trade; for deduplication or compliance it may not be.

**Embedding models are asymmetric.** Some expect a prefix like `query:` or `passage:` on each side. Skip it and similarity quietly degrades — no error, just worse answers.

</Deeper>

</Section>

<Section n="05" label="Where it breaks" title="What semantic search is bad at.">

- **Exact things.** Product codes, error strings, names, IDs. Embeddings blur exactly the details you need; a string match would have won.
- **The model’s blind spots.** If the embedding model never learned your domain, “similar” is confidently wrong. Rare jargon is the usual casualty.
- **Chunking damage.** Split a table from its caption, a clause from its contract, and retrieval can only find half of the meaning.
- **Top-k without reranking.** Close is not the same as correct. The right document can sit at rank 6 when you asked for 5.
- **Cost and drift.** Every document must be embedded once, and re-embedded whenever the model changes. That migration is real money and real time.
- **Evaluation.** Without a small set of real queries and known-good answers, you are tuning by vibe. A number you don’t measure is a number you can’t improve.

This is why production search is usually hybrid: word-based ranking for the exact things, vectors for the fuzzy things, a reranker to settle the argument.

</Section>
